## Overall strategy

I donate to causes in the hope that it will help solve some of humanity’s problems. If humanity’s problems were a disease, then I like to imagine my donation strategy as a two-pronged approach: treating the symptoms, and finding the cure.

“Treating the symptoms” includes things like:
- Preventing and treating Malaria, a disease which is easily prevented and treated, yet kills hundreds of thousands of people each year
- Giving money (with no strings attached) to the poorest families in the world
- Humanitarian aid during war

“Finding the cure” includes things like:
- Cancer and aging research
- Climate change solutions
- Improving the quality and accessibility of education
- Preventing AI apocalypse

The distinction is not arbitrary. Donations to “treat the symptoms” will very quickly alleviate suffering, and there is usually very little uncertainty about how additional funding will make an impact. On the other hand, “finding the cure” can take decades, and the path is much less certain. 

If you have a disease, you can’t just ignore your symptoms and focus all your energy in searching for the cure, since the cure may take a long time to find, and you may suffer a long time from your symptoms. Similarly, I can’t ignore the poverty and famine that plague the world today, especially when my donations could quickly alleviate someone’s suffering. And if the world ends tomorrow, all that cure-finding would’ve been for nothing, but at least I did some real good.

Conversely, if we only focus on “treating the symptoms,” we will never solve the big problems that beset us. Some of these problems (like aging) will eventually kill us; others (like unaligned superintelligence) may eradicate the human race. 

Where the analogy breaks down is that “finding the cure” doesn’t necessarily “make the symptoms disappear.” There may always be less fortunate people in the world who need urgent help. That’s why part of my donations go toward “treating the symptoms.” The other part goes toward “finding the cure,” because if we solve some of these important problems, we will have made progress as a species, and that progress is ratcheting.

How much, then, to allocate to each part? I divide one third for the “symptoms,”and two thirds for the “cure.” I do this because I’m not purely altruistic, and I want to know that a majority of my donations will eventually benefit me and my family. “Finding the cure” benefits everyone on Earth and future generations, while “treating the symptoms”—although important, as explained above—only benefits strangers in the present. 

## Current recipients and rationale

“Treating the symptoms” goes to GiveWell’s Top Charities Fund. I think this fund is the best way to do the most good given limited resources. GiveWell estimates that it [costs around $3000](https://www.givewell.org/how-much-does-it-cost-to-save-a-life) to save someone’s life. 

“Finding the cure” goes toward AI safety (MIRI, METR, and CAIS. Why these in particular? That’s a subject for another post :smile:). Why AI safety, as opposed to other big problems? Because I think it’s the most under-funded relative to potential impact:

| Cause | Potential impact | Cumulative funding (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| AI safety | Humanity does not cease to exist | [Around 1 billion](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/WGpFFJo2uFe5ssgEb/an-overview-of-the-ai-safety-funding-situation) |
| Aging/cancer research | Tens of millions of people who die each year can be saved | [Hundreds of billions](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-02609-2) on cancer research alone |
| Climate change | I don’t know, but less than aging | [Tens of billions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITER) on nuclear fusion alone |
| Education | Instrumental to the other causes | I don’t know, but I worked in Ed Tech for 4 years, which maybe substitutes donating for now |

From this comparison, it would seem that AI safety has the greatest potential impact, but is the least funded (ignoring education, which I've excused myself from).
