Bayesian Analysis of METR Data

I re-analyze METR's data on LLM capabilities using a Bayesian approach.
May 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Introduction#

The capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have been improving rapidly, and measuring the pace of progress is crucial for informing AI policy decisions. To quantify the capabilities of AI models, an independent non-profit Model Evaluation and Threat Research (METR) evaluated whether various models could complete complex tasks. Extrapolating, they predict that in under a decade, AI will be able to complete tasks that take human experts weeks or months to complete 1.

The METR analysis was originally done using frequentist methods. However, the METR data is particularly suitable for Bayesian methods because of the hierarchical structure and relatively small amount of data. In this project, I re-analyse the data using a Bayesian approach, comparing three hierarchical models and reporting the capability trend.

Background#

METR analysis#

The METR analysis was done as follows. First, they collect a set of 170 tasks of varying difficulty, ranging from things like finding a fact on Wikipedia to training an image model. To quantify the difficulty of a task, they record the amount of time it takes for human experts to complete it.

Next, they collect a set of 33 LLMs that were released over the past few years (e.g. GPT-3, GPT-4, etc). Each model attempts to complete each task using an agentic framework, and the success or failure of each attempt is recorded. For each model iii, a logistic regression is fitted to predict the model’s success or failure, given task difficulty:

pij=σ(wilog⁡2tj+bi) p_{ij} = \sigma(w_i\log_2 t_j + b_i) pij​=σ(wi​log2​tj​+bi​)

where tjt_jtj​ is the mean number of minutes it takes for a human to complete task jjj, and pijp_{ij}pij​ is the predicted probability that model iii succeeds at task jjj.

The capability of a model cic_ici​ is then defined as the task difficulty (in log-minutes) corresponding to the predicted 50% success rate:

0.5=σ(wici+bi)⇒ci=−biwi \begin{align*} 0.5 &= \sigma(w_i c_i + b_i) \\ \Rightarrow c_i &= -\frac{b_i}{w_i} \end{align*} 0.5⇒ci​​=σ(wi​ci​+bi​)=−wi​bi​​​

Finally, a linear regression is performed to identify the trend between model capability and model release date.

Bayesian re-analysis of METR data#

Recent work by Moss 2 also re-analyzes the METR data using Bayesian methods, comparing different functions modeling the relationship between capability and release date. Key differences between their work and mine are:

  • I use different, smaller parameterizations in an effort to more closely follow METR’s original approach.
  • I perform posterior predictive checks and use PSIS-LOO-CV to assess the benefits of adding more parameters.
  • Their implementation is in Stan, while mine is in PyMC.

Method#

I compare three different models:

  1. Basic model: A straightforward Bayesian reformulation of the original frequentist approach by METR
  2. Model with task-level variables: Introduces additional task-level noise to the basic model
  3. Piecewise linear model: Models the capability trend as a piecewise linear function

Basic model#

This hierarchical model is a straightforward Bayesian reformulation of the original frequentist approach by METR.

At the top of the hierarchical model is a Bayesian simple linear regression. It models the relationship between model capability cic_ici​ and model release date, where δi\delta_iδi​ is the model release date (in days, centered on the mean and scaled to have variance 1).

α0∼N(0,τ=1)α1∼N(0,τ=1)τc∼Exp(1)ci∼N(α0+α1δi,τc) \begin{align*} \alpha_0 &\sim N(0, \tau=1) \\ \alpha_1 &\sim N(0, \tau=1) \\ \tau_c &\sim \mathrm{Exp}(1) \\ c_i &\sim N(\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \delta_i, \tau_c) \end{align*} α0​α1​τc​ci​​∼N(0,τ=1)∼N(0,τ=1)∼Exp(1)∼N(α0​+α1​δi​,τc​)​

The original logistic regression can be rewritten in terms of cic_ici​ (the task difficulty in log-minutes corresponding to the predicted 50% success rate):

pij=invlogit(wi′(log⁡2tj−ci)) p_{ij} = \mathrm{invlogit}(w_i'(\log_2 t_j - c_i)) pij​=invlogit(wi′​(log2​tj​−ci​))

We know wi′<0w_i' < 0wi′​<0 since LLMs succeed at shorter tasks and fail at longer tasks. So it can be modeled as wi′=−eβiw_i' = -e^{\beta_i}wi′​=−eβi​ with a normal prior on βi\beta_iβi​. That gives the following likelihood:

βi∼N(0,τ=1)pij=invlogit(eβi(ci−log⁡2tj))Xij∼Bin(nij,pij) \begin{align*} \beta_i &\sim N(0, \tau=1) \\ p_{ij} &= \mathrm{invlogit}\left(e^{\beta_i} (c_i - \log_2 t_j)\right) \\ X_{ij} &\sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_{ij}, p_{ij}) \end{align*} βi​pij​Xij​​∼N(0,τ=1)=invlogit(eβi​(ci​−log2​tj​))∼Bin(nij​,pij​)​

where nijn_{ij}nij​ is the number of times model iii attempted task jjj, and XijX_{ij}Xij​ is the number of successes. There are some (model, task) combinations that do not have any attempts recorded, so they should not be included in the likelihood; in practice, XijX_{ij}Xij​ and nijn_{ij}nij​ are set to 0 so that the likelihood is constant (with respect to parameters) for that combination.

I also standardize log⁡2tj\log_2 t_jlog2​tj​ so that it has mean 0 and variance 1 before inputting it to the model.

Model with per-task variables#

The basic model above does not account for variance in task difficulty, since the METR data only reports the mean of the time taken per task. In the original work by METR, this problem is mitigated by estimating variance through bootstrap sampling.

For a Bayesian reformulation, we should account for this variance using additional variables. Instead of inputting the task time tjt_jtj​ directly, I add some noise to get the task difficulty djd_jdj​:

τd∼Exp(1)dj∼N(log⁡2tj,τd)pij=invlogit(eβi(ci−dj)) \begin{align*} \tau_d &\sim \mathrm{Exp}(1) \\ d_j &\sim N(\log_2 t_j, \tau_d) \\ p_{ij} &= \mathrm{invlogit}\left(e^{\beta_i} (c_i - d_j)\right) \end{align*} τd​dj​pij​​∼Exp(1)∼N(log2​tj​,τd​)=invlogit(eβi​(ci​−dj​))​

Sampling#

I use the NUTS sampler with 4 chains and target accept rate 0.8. For the basic model, I use 1000 tuning samples and 2000 draws. The model with per-task variables requires more samples due to the higher number of parameters: 2000 for tuning and 10,000 for sampling.

Model fit and selection#

To compare the two models, I first perform posterior predictive checks to see how well the models follow the observed data. I also use PSIS-LOO-CV to assess the tradeoff between increasing the number of parameters and any log-likelihood gains.
